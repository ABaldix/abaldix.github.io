The Solar Installation Process: Step-by-Step Timeline

From the first quote to permission to operate, going solar usually takes 1–3 months, while the physical installation takes just 1–3 days. Each step explained, with typical timelines and tips to avoid delays.

VivaVoltia Editorial Team Updated October 30, 2026 · 3 min read

Key Takeaways

  • Most projects take about 1–3 months from signed contract to switch-on; the rooftop work itself usually takes 1–3 days.
  • Permits, inspections, and utility approval cause most of the wait.
  • Don't turn the system on until the utility grants permission to operate (PTO).
  • Tie payments to milestones, with the final payment due at PTO.

Many homeowners expect the solar installation process to take weeks of noisy construction. In reality, crews are usually on your roof for only a day or two. Most of the timeline is paperwork: design, permits, inspections, and utility approval. Here's what happens at each stage and how long it typically takes.

Solar Installation Process Timeline at a Glance

StageTypical durationWho's responsible
1. Quotes and contract1–4 weeksYou
2. Site survey1 visit (1–3 hours)Installer
3. Design and engineering1–2 weeksInstaller
4. Permits, HOA, and utility application1–6 weeksInstaller (you for HOA)
5. Installation1–3 daysInstaller
6. Inspection1–3 weeksLocal building department
7. Permission to operate (PTO)1–6 weeksUtility
 Timelines vary widely by city and utility. Total from contract to PTO is commonly 1–3 months.

Step 1: Get Quotes and Sign a Contract

Collect at least three quotes and compare them carefully; our quote checklist covers what to look for. Before signing, confirm the exact equipment, total price, payment schedule, warranties, and what happens if the roof needs work.

Step 2: Site Survey

A technician visits to measure the roof, check its structure and condition, inspect your electrical panel, and confirm shading. If your main panel is too small or full, this is when a panel upgrade will be identified.

Step 3: Design and Engineering

The installer produces a final layout and electrical design, and sometimes a structural letter from an engineer. You should approve the final design before permits are filed. Check that it matches the quote.

Step 4: Permits, HOA Approval, and Interconnection

The installer applies for a building and/or electrical permit and submits an interconnection application to your utility. If you have an HOA, submit your architectural application early; see HOA rules. Some cities now use NREL's SolarAPP+, an automated tool that can issue permits for standard residential systems instantly.

Step 5: Installation Day

A typical crew installs a residential system in one to three days:

  1. Attaching mounts and racking to the roof, with flashing to keep water out.
  2. Mounting panels and module-level electronics, if used.
  3. Running conduit and wiring to the inverter and your electrical panel.
  4. Installing the inverter, battery (if any), monitoring, and disconnects.

Batteries, panel upgrades, or complex roofs can add a day or two.

Step 6: Inspection

A local building inspector checks that the work meets code. Some jurisdictions also require a fire department inspection. Your installer schedules this and usually attends.

Step 7: Permission to Operate

After inspection, the utility reviews the paperwork, may install or reprogram your meter, and issues permission to operate (PTO). Only then should the system be switched on. Turning it on early can violate your interconnection agreement.

Payment tip: a fair schedule ties payments to milestones, for example a small deposit at signing, a portion at installation, and the balance at PTO. Be cautious about paying most of the price upfront.

How to Avoid Delays

  • Have 12 months of utility bills ready.
  • Start the HOA application as soon as the design is final.
  • Respond quickly to document requests from the installer or utility.
  • Ask the installer for their typical permit and PTO times in your city.
  • Fix roof issues before installation day.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to be home on installation day?

Usually only at the start and end, and to provide access to the electrical panel and attic if needed. Power may be off for a few hours.

What happens after PTO?

You'll get monitoring access and documentation. Keep your contract, warranties, permits, and PTO letter; you'll need them for warranty claims, insurance, and selling your home.
