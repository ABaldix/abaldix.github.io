How Much Does a Solar Battery Cost in 2026?

Home batteries cost about $10,000–$16,000 installed for 10–15 kWh in 2026, with no federal credit for purchases. Prices by brand and size, what drives the cost, and how to tell if a battery will pay off.

VivaVoltia Editorial Team Updated October 30, 2026 · 3 min read

Key Takeaways

  • A typical 10–15 kWh home battery costs about $10,000–$16,000 installed in 2026.
  • Installed prices generally fall between $850 and $1,300 per kWh of usable capacity; second units cost less per kWh.
  • Purchased batteries installed in 2026 get no federal tax credit; state programs like California's SGIP can help.
  • Batteries pay off fastest under net billing or time-of-use rates, or when backup power has high value to you.

Home batteries have become much more common as utilities cut export credits and outages become more frequent. They are also expensive, and in 2026 there is no federal tax credit for buying one. Here's what a battery really costs, what drives the price, and how to decide whether it's worth it.

Industry data in 2026 puts installed costs at roughly $850–$1,300 per kWh of usable capacity. Adding a second unit at the same time usually costs less per kWh, because much of the labor, permitting, and electrical work is shared.

What Drives the Price

  • Capacity (kWh): how much energy it stores.
  • Power (kW): how many appliances it can run at once. Higher power lets you start central AC or a well pump.
  • Whole-home vs. partial backup: backing up only critical circuits is cheaper.
  • Electrical work: main panel upgrades or a separate critical-loads panel can add $1,500–$4,000.
  • Coupling: DC-coupled batteries with hybrid inverters can be cheaper for new systems; AC-coupled batteries are easier to retrofit. See hybrid vs. grid-tie inverters.

Incentives for Batteries in 2026

The 30% federal credit that covered batteries of 3 kWh or more ended for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025. What's left:

  • California SGIP: battery incentives, with larger amounts for eligible low-income, medically vulnerable, and high-fire-risk households, subject to funding.
  • Utility "virtual power plant" programs: some utilities pay you upfront or per event to use your battery during peak demand.
  • State programs in places such as Massachusetts, Oregon, and others; see incentives by state.
  • Leased batteries: the owner may still claim the federal business credit.

Does a Battery Pay for Itself?

Under full retail net metering, a battery rarely pays for itself on bill savings alone, because the grid already credits your exports at full value. Batteries make more financial sense when:

  • Your utility uses net billing with low export credits, as in California under NEM 3.0.
  • You're on time-of-use rates with expensive evening peaks.
  • You can earn money from a utility battery program.
  • You place real value on backup power.

Most lithium home batteries carry 10-year warranties, often guaranteeing about 70% of capacity at the end. Plan on the battery lasting 10–15 years, less than your panels.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I add a battery now or later?

Installing together usually saves on labor and permitting, and a hybrid inverter can make it cheaper. If budget is tight, choose equipment that makes adding a battery easy later.

Are LFP batteries better?

For homes, LFP (lithium iron phosphate) is now standard because it's more thermally stable and handles more cycles, though it's slightly heavier.
