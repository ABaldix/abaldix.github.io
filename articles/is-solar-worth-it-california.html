Is Solar Worth It in California in 2026? Costs, NEM 3.0, and Payback

California pays some of the highest electricity prices in the country, but NEM 3.0 cut the value of exported solar. Here is the 2026 math for solar-only and solar-plus-battery systems, and who comes out ahead.

VivaVoltia Editorial Team Updated October 30, 2026 · 5 min read

Key Takeaways

  • California's average residential price was 29.8¢/kWh in August 2026, about 64% above the US average, so every solar kWh you use at home is valuable.
  • Under NEM 3.0, exported solar earns roughly a quarter of retail on average, so solar-only systems pay back in about 9 years instead of 5.
  • Adding a battery raises the upfront cost but roughly doubles how much solar you use yourself, and usually increases 25-year savings.
  • There is no federal credit for purchased systems after 2025; leases and PPAs can still pass through the commercial credit.

Is solar worth it in California? For most homeowners who own their roof and pay PG&E, SCE, or SDG&E rates, yes, but the reason has changed. Under the old net metering rules, solar paid off by selling power back to the grid. Under NEM 3.0, it pays off by replacing the expensive electricity you would otherwise buy, especially in the evening. That makes system design, and often a battery, far more important than it used to be.

How NEM 3.0 Changes the Math

If you applied to interconnect on or after April 15, 2023, you are on the Net Billing Tariff. Power you use the moment it is produced still offsets your full retail rate. Power you send to the grid earns an export credit based on the utility's "avoided cost," which changes by hour and month. Midday credits in spring are often just a few cents; late-summer evening credits can be much higher. Averaged over a year, exports are worth roughly a quarter of retail for a typical solar-only home.

The rules are now settled: in March 2026 a state appeals court upheld the tariff, and in June 2026 the California Supreme Court declined to review it. We explain the details in our NEM 3.0 guide.

Already on NEM 1.0 or 2.0? You keep your original terms for 20 years from your interconnection date. Adding a large amount of new solar can move you to the new tariff, so ask your installer before expanding.

Is Solar Worth It in California? A 2026 Example

We ran our solar calculator for a home using 900 kWh a month at California's average price, with an installed cost of $2.85 per watt and no federal credit. The solar-only case assumes 40% of the output is used on site and the rest is exported at about 25% of retail. The battery case assumes a 13.5 kWh battery adds about $14,000 and lifts on-site use to roughly 85%.

The pattern is what matters: solar alone still pays for itself, but a battery that stores midday power for the 4 p.m. to 9 p.m. peak captures much more of California's high rates. If you qualify for an SGIP storage rebate, the battery case improves further.

Utility, Rate Plan, and Climate Zone Matter

  • SDG&E customers usually pay the highest rates in the state, which shortens payback.
  • PG&E and SCE customers on time-of-use plans benefit most from evening battery discharge.
  • Municipal utilities such as LADWP and SMUD set their own export rules and lower rates, so the numbers above do not apply directly.
  • Inland and desert areas produce noticeably more per panel than the foggy coast, and also use more air conditioning in the evening.

Incentives That Still Apply in California

With the federal homeowner credit gone, California's remaining help is narrower:

  • SGIP battery rebates. The Self-Generation Incentive Program pays per watt-hour of storage, with larger amounts for income-qualified and wildfire-risk households. Budgets open and close, so ask installers what is available now.
  • Property tax exclusion. New solar is excluded from reassessment under state law, so your property tax does not rise because of the system.
  • Leases and PPAs. Third-party owners can still claim the commercial credit under the current deadlines and may pass part of it on in a lower price. Compare carefully using our lease vs. buy guide.

Who Should Go Solar in California, and Who Should Wait

Solar is usually a strong choice if you own a home with a sunny, south- or west-facing roof that will not need replacing for 15+ years, you use a meaningful amount of power in the evening (EV charging, air conditioning, electric cooking), and you plan to stay long enough to pass the payback point. It is weaker if your usage is very low, your roof is heavily shaded, or you expect to move within a few years. West-facing panels deserve a fresh look under NEM 3.0, because they produce more in the late afternoon when power is worth most. See our guide to orientation and tilt.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a battery with solar in California?

Not strictly, but under NEM 3.0 most new systems include one because exported power earns so little. A battery shifts midday solar into the evening peak, which is where most of the savings now come from.

How long does solar take to pay off in California?

For a typical purchased system in 2026, roughly 8 to 11 years depending on your utility, rate plan, usage pattern, and whether you add storage. Very high users in SDG&E territory can do better.

Is NEM 3.0 going to change again?

The tariff survived its legal challenges in 2026, so it is the rule for new customers for now. Export credit values are updated as the CPUC's avoided-cost model is refreshed, which can move them up or down over time.

Can I still get a tax credit for solar in California?

Not for a system you buy and place in service after December 31, 2025. Leases and power purchase agreements can still benefit indirectly through the commercial credit that the owner claims.
