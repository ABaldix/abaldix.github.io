How Much Do Solar Panels Cost for a 2,000 Sq Ft House in 2026?

A typical 2,000 sq ft home needs a 7–9 kW system costing about $19,000–$25,000 before incentives in 2026. A full cost breakdown, battery add-on prices, and realistic payback periods.

VivaVoltia Editorial Team Updated October 30, 2026 · 4 min read

Key Takeaways

  • Most 2,000 sq ft homes need 7–9 kW of solar, or about 17–22 panels.
  • At $2.60–$3.00 per watt, that is roughly $19,000–$25,000 before incentives in 2026.
  • There is no federal credit for homeowner purchases in 2026; state incentives can still cut costs.
  • Typical payback is 9–13 years, faster where electricity is expensive.

"How much will solar cost for my house?" is the first question most homeowners ask. Square footage is only a rough guide, because what really sets system size is how much electricity you use. Still, a typical 2,000 square foot home falls into a predictable range, and this guide breaks down what you can expect to pay in 2026.

The Short Answer

A typical 2,000 sq ft home in the US uses around 10,000–12,000 kWh of electricity per year, which calls for a 7–9 kW solar system. At 2026 installed prices of roughly $2.60–$3.00 per watt, that works out to about:

Why Square Footage Is Only a Starting Point

Two 2,000 sq ft homes can have very different electricity bills. These factors matter more than size:

  • Climate and cooling: a home in Phoenix running air conditioning all summer can use twice as much power as the same home in coastal Oregon.
  • Heating type: electric heat pumps or baseboard heat add a lot of winter usage; gas heat does not.
  • Electric vehicles: an EV typically adds 3,000–4,500 kWh per year. See solar and EV charging.
  • Sunshine: 1 kW of panels produces about 1,500+ kWh per year in the Southwest and closer to 1,100 kWh in the Northeast and Pacific Northwest.

The most accurate approach is to total the kWh from your last 12 utility bills and use our sizing guide or solar calculator.

Where the Money Goes

Hardware is now a minority of the price. On a typical residential installation:

  • Panels: roughly 15–20% of the total.
  • Inverter(s): roughly 10%.
  • Racking, wiring, and electrical parts: roughly 10%.
  • Labor, permitting, inspection, design, sales, and overhead ("soft costs"): about half or more.

That is why prices vary so much between installers for similar equipment, and why getting several quotes pays off. See how to compare solar quotes.

What Happened to the 30% Federal Tax Credit?

Until 2025, homeowners who bought a system could claim a 30% federal tax credit, which would have cut an 8 kW, $22,800 system to about $15,960. That credit was repealed by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and ended on December 31, 2025. In 2026, the gross price is what you pay, minus any state or utility incentives. Read more in our tax credit guide.

State programs can still help significantly. In New York, for example, a 25% state tax credit (up to $5,000) plus NY-Sun rebates can offset a large share of the cost. See incentives by state.

What If You Add a Battery?

A single battery such as a Tesla Powerwall 3 (13.5 kWh) typically adds about $12,000–$16,000 installed, and there is no federal credit for batteries bought in 2026. A battery adds backup power during outages and makes more sense where utilities pay little for exported solar. See solar battery costs and whether you need one.

Is It Worth the Investment?

For a 2,000 sq ft home spending $150–$200 a month on electricity, an 8 kW system costing about $22,000 typically pays for itself in roughly 9 to 13 years without incentives, assuming electricity prices keep rising about 3% a year. Payback is faster in states with high rates or strong incentives and slower where power is cheap. With panels warrantied for 25 years, that can still leave more than a decade of very low-cost electricity.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is $3 per watt a good price?

It's within the normal range for 2026, but quotes closer to $2.50–$2.75 per watt for standard equipment are common in competitive markets. Compare at least three quotes on a per-watt basis.

Can I install solar myself to save money?

DIY can cut costs by roughly $1 per watt, but it requires permits, electrical inspections, and utility approval, and it can affect warranties. Most homeowners hire a licensed installer.

Do bigger systems cost less per watt?

Usually, yes. Fixed costs such as permits and design are spread over more panels, so the price per watt tends to fall as size increases.
