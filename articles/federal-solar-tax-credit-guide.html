The 30% Federal Solar Tax Credit Ended: What Homeowners Need to Know in 2026

☀️

By the VivaVoltia Editorial Team

Last reviewed: October 2026

For almost twenty years, the biggest financial incentive for going solar in the United States was the federal solar tax credit. Homeowners who bought a system could subtract 30% of its cost from their federal income tax. That credit is gone for systems installed in 2026. Here is what changed, who can still benefit, and what incentives remain.

What Changed?

The homeowner credit was officially called the Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D). The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 had extended it at 30% until 2032. In July 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (H.R. 1) repealed that schedule and set a hard end date: December 31, 2025, with no gradual phase-down.

  • Installed and placed in service in 2025 or earlier: eligible for the 30% credit.
  • Bought with cash or a loan in 2026 or later: no federal credit.
  • Home batteries bought in 2026: no federal credit either; batteries were covered by the same Section 25D credit.

I Installed Solar in 2025. Can I Still Claim It?

Yes. If your system was completed and operating by December 31, 2025, you claim the credit on your 2025 tax return by filing IRS Form 5695 with your Form 1040. The credit is non-refundable, which means it reduces the tax you owe but does not generate a refund on its own. If the credit is larger than your tax bill, the unused amount can be carried forward to future tax years.

Keep your paperwork: Save your installation contract, final invoice, and the utility's permission-to-operate letter. These show when the system was placed in service, which is what decides eligibility.

Is There Any Federal Credit Left for Home Solar?

Indirectly, yes. The 2025 law kept a separate business credit, Section 48E, which companies can claim on systems they own. When you sign a solar lease or PPA, the solar company owns the panels on your roof and may claim that credit. Projects generally qualify if construction began by July 4, 2026, or if the system is placed in service by December 31, 2027.

You don't receive this credit directly. Some companies pass part of the saving on through lower monthly payments, but there is no guarantee. Read our solar lease vs. buy comparison before you sign.

Incentives That Still Exist

  • State rebates and tax credits: several states run their own solar or battery programs. Check your state energy office or the DSIRE database (dsireusa.org).
  • Property tax exemptions: many states exclude the added value of solar from your property assessment.
  • Net metering: credits for excess power you send to the grid. Rules vary by utility; see our net metering guide.
  • SRECs: in some states you can sell solar renewable energy certificates for extra income.
  • Utility battery programs: some utilities pay you to share stored battery power during peak demand.

Is Solar Still Worth It Without the Credit?

For many households it still is, especially in states with high electricity prices. Losing a 30% credit makes payback periods longer, often 10 to 12 years instead of 6 to 8. Panels typically carry 25-year warranties, so there are still years of savings after payback. Our "Are solar panels worth it?" guide walks through the factors that matter most.
