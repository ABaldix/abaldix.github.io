Is Solar Worth It in North Carolina in 2026? Duke Rates, PowerPair, and Payback

North Carolina has good sun but low electricity prices, and Duke Energy changed its solar rules in 2023. Here is how the 2026 options compare and when solar still pays.

VivaVoltia Editorial Team Updated October 30, 2026 · 4 min read

Key Takeaways

  • North Carolina's average residential price was 14.9¢/kWh in August 2026, below the US average, which stretches payback.
  • New Duke Energy customers choose between the Net Metering Bridge Rate, open until 2027, and the time-of-use Residential Solar Choice rate.
  • Duke's PowerPair program offers up to $9,000 for new solar-plus-battery systems while capacity lasts.
  • A solar-only system typically pays back in about 12 to 14 years; Dominion customers still get 1:1 net metering.

Is solar worth it in North Carolina? It can be, but it is a closer call than in high-price states. North Carolina has solid sunshine and a large, experienced installer market, yet residential electricity costs less than the national average. Duke Energy, which serves most of the state, also replaced classic net metering for new customers in 2023. The best results now come from picking the right rate plan and, often, pairing solar with a battery.

Duke Energy's Two Options for New Solar Customers

Duke Energy Carolinas and Duke Energy Progress customers who applied after October 2023 choose between two riders:

  • Net Metering Bridge Rate. A transitional, modified form of net metering that you can lock in for 15 years. It is available to new customers until 2027 and is subject to annual caps. It is simpler and usually more valuable for solar-only systems.
  • Residential Solar Choice. A time-of-use rate where exports are credited at lower values and power is priced by time of day. It pays less for solar alone but rewards a battery that covers your evening peak.

Both options include monthly minimum charges that solar cannot offset. Customers who applied before October 2023 keep legacy net metering through 2027.

Duke PowerPair: Up to $9,000 for Solar Plus Battery

PowerPair is a one-time incentive for new Duke customers who install solar and a battery together. It pays $0.36 per watt of solar (up to 10 kW, or $3,600) and $400 per kWh of storage (up to 13.5 kWh, or $5,400). Solar-only systems, battery-only systems, and add-ons to existing solar do not qualify. Participants can also earn monthly bill credits by letting Duke use their battery during peak events. The program has limited capacity, so ask installers whether funding is still open before you sign.

Other North Carolina Incentives

  • Property tax. North Carolina excludes residential solar energy systems from property tax, so your assessment does not rise.
  • Leases and PPAs. Third-party owners may still claim the commercial credit, but North Carolina limits some third-party arrangements, so availability depends on your utility.
  • Co-ops and municipal utilities. Many have their own net metering or net billing rules and occasional rebates. Search DSIRE by ZIP code.

Who Should Go Solar in North Carolina

Solar makes the most sense if you have a sunny roof that will last another 15+ years, a high summer bill, and plans to stay at least 12 years. Dominion customers have the easiest case. Duke customers should compare the Bridge Rate against Solar Choice with a battery, and decide whether backup power during hurricanes and ice storms is worth paying for. Frequently Asked Questions

Does Duke Energy still offer net metering in North Carolina?

Not classic net metering for new customers. New customers can choose the Net Metering Bridge Rate, a modified net metering option open until 2027, or the time-of-use Residential Solar Choice rate.

How much is the Duke PowerPair rebate?

Up to $9,000: $0.36 per watt of solar up to 10 kW, plus $400 per kWh of battery up to 13.5 kWh, for new solar-plus-battery installations while capacity remains.

Is solar worth it in North Carolina without a battery?

It can be, especially on the Bridge Rate or with Dominion net metering, but expect a payback of roughly 12 to 14 years at average prices.

Do solar panels raise property taxes in North Carolina?

No. Residential solar energy systems are excluded from property tax in North Carolina.
