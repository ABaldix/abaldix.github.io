Do Solar Panels Work During a Power Outage?

Most grid-tied solar systems shut off during a blackout for safety. Why that happens, the three ways to keep your lights on with solar, and how much backup power costs.

VivaVoltia Editorial Team Updated October 30, 2026 · 3 min read

Key Takeaways

  • Standard grid-tied systems automatically shut down during outages to protect utility workers.
  • To use solar in a blackout you need a battery with backup capability or a specially designed inverter.
  • A single 10–14 kWh battery can keep essentials running for roughly a day, longer with solar recharging.
  • Some inverters offer a small daytime-only backup outlet, a low-cost partial solution.

Many homeowners are surprised to learn that their solar panels stop working when the power goes out, even on a sunny day. It's a deliberate safety feature, not a malfunction. Here's why it happens and what you can do to keep the lights on.

Why Grid-Tied Systems Shut Down in Outages

Most residential systems in the US are grid-tied: they're connected to the utility grid so you can use net metering. When the grid goes down, line workers are repairing lines they expect to be de-energized. If your panels kept pushing power onto those lines, they could injure or kill someone.

To prevent that, grid-tied inverters must have anti-islanding protection under the UL 1741 and IEEE 1547 standards that utilities require for interconnection. When the inverter detects the grid is down, it stops exporting power within a fraction of a second. Without a battery or a special backup setup, that means your home loses solar power too.

Don't try to bypass it. Any installer who offers to disable anti-islanding on a standard grid-tied system is creating a serious safety hazard and violating code.

Three Ways to Use Solar During an Outage

1. Add a battery with backup capability

A battery with a transfer switch or gateway disconnects your home from the grid during an outage, creating a safe "island." Your panels keep running and recharge the battery during the day, and the battery powers your home at night. This is the most complete solution.

2. Use an inverter with a daytime backup outlet

Some string inverters include an outlet that delivers limited power (often around 1.5–2 kW) from the panels during daylight in an outage. It won't run your house, but it can charge phones, run a refrigerator, or power a few devices while the sun shines.

3. Pair solar with a generator or portable power station

A standby generator can cover long outages, while a portable power station can recharge from small portable panels. Neither uses your rooftop system directly. See portable power stations vs. home batteries.

How Much Battery Do You Need for Backup?

Start by listing what you want to keep running. A refrigerator, lights, internet, a furnace fan, and phone charging use roughly 10–15 kWh per day, which one typical battery covers, especially with solar recharging during the day. Central air conditioning, electric heat, and well pumps need more power and capacity. Many installers set up a critical loads panel so the battery powers only selected circuits, stretching runtime. See how to size batteries.

Is Backup Power Worth It?

That depends on how often you lose power and what an outage costs you. For homes with medical equipment, well water, home offices, or frequent outages from storms, wildfire shutoffs, or heat waves, a battery can be well worth it. A battery also adds bill savings where utilities pay little for exported solar. See solar with or without a battery.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I add a battery to my existing solar system?

Usually, yes. AC-coupled batteries work with almost any existing system. Your installer will check inverter compatibility and your electrical panel.

Do off-grid systems work in outages?

Yes. Off-grid systems aren't connected to the utility, so outages don't affect them, but they need much larger batteries and often a generator.
