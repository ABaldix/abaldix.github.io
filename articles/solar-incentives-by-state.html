Solar Incentives by State in 2026: What's Left After the Federal Credit

With the federal homeowner credit gone, state programs decide much of solar's value. The main state tax credits, rebates, SREC markets, and tax exemptions in 2026, and how to find yours.

VivaVoltia Editorial Team Updated October 30, 2026 · 4 min read

Key Takeaways

  • About a dozen states offer their own solar tax credits or rebates; New York's 25% credit (up to $5,000) plus NY-Sun is among the strongest.
  • SREC and performance-payment programs in states like New Jersey, Massachusetts, and Illinois pay you for the power you produce.
  • Property and sales tax exemptions are common and quietly save hundreds to thousands of dollars.
  • Program funding and rules change often, so confirm current terms in DSIRE and with your state energy office before you sign.

Since the federal homeowner credit ended on December 31, 2025, solar incentives by state matter more than ever. State tax credits, rebates, payments for the energy you produce, tax exemptions, and net metering rules can easily make a difference of $5,000 or more over the life of a system. This guide explains the main types of incentives and highlights the states with the strongest programs.

Always confirm before you sign. State programs often work in funding "blocks" that close when money runs out, and rules change between legislative sessions. Use the figures here as a starting point, then check the official program page and the DSIRE database.

The Five Types of State Solar Incentives

1. State income tax credits

A state credit reduces the state income tax you owe, just as the old federal credit did for federal tax. They are usually capped and are only worth the full amount if you owe enough state tax.

2. Upfront rebates

Rebates lower the price at installation, often paid to the installer and passed on to you. They are commonly set per watt and decline as more people sign up.

3. Performance payments and SRECs

Some states pay you for the clean energy your system produces, either through solar renewable energy certificates (SRECs) or fixed-price programs. One SREC usually represents 1 MWh of solar production.

4. Property and sales tax exemptions

Many states exclude the value added by solar from your property tax assessment and waive sales tax on equipment. These don't make headlines, but they help.

5. Net metering

Not a subsidy in the strict sense, but full retail-rate credit for exported power is one of the most valuable policies a state can have. See how net metering works.

What If My State Has No Incentives?

Several states, including Texas, have no statewide solar credit or rebate. Solar can still make sense there if your electricity rate is high or your utility offers a buyback plan, and some local utilities and cities run their own rebates. Austin Energy and CPS Energy in Texas are long-running examples. Search DSIRE by ZIP code to see utility-level programs, which statewide summaries often miss.

Utility Programs Worth Asking About

  • Battery or "virtual power plant" programs that pay you to let the utility use your battery during peak demand.
  • Time-of-use rate plans that make solar plus storage more valuable.
  • Low-income solar programs, often with much larger rebates for qualifying households.
  • Community solar, if your roof isn't suitable: you subscribe to part of a local solar farm and receive bill credits.

How to Find Every Incentive You Qualify For

  1. Search your ZIP code on DSIRE (dsireusa.org).
  2. Check your state energy office website for current funding levels.
  3. Call your utility and ask about net metering, rate plans, and battery programs.
  4. Ask each installer to list every incentive in the quote, with the program name, and how and when the money is paid.
  5. Confirm tax credits with a tax professional, especially if your state tax bill is small.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I combine state credits and rebates?

Usually yes, but some states reduce the credit basis by the amount of a rebate. Check each program's rules.

Do state incentives apply to leased systems?

Often they go to the system owner, which is the leasing company. Ask how they are reflected in your lease price.

Are incentives taxable?

It depends on the program. Rebates may reduce your cost basis, and income from SRECs may be taxable. A tax professional can confirm.
