Solar panels and electric vehicles are a natural pair: the car turns cheap sunshine into miles, and the extra usage makes a solar system more valuable. If you are thinking about charging an EV with solar panels, here's how to size your system and get the most from it.

How Much Electricity Does an EV Use?

Most EVs travel about 3 to 4 miles per kWh, according to EPA ratings on FuelEconomy.gov; larger SUVs and trucks are less efficient. Home charging also loses about 10% of energy along the way.

How Many Extra Panels Do You Need?

At 2026 prices of about $2.60–$3.00 per watt, adding 3 kW for an EV costs roughly $8,000–$9,000, often less when it's part of a larger system.

Is Charging With Solar Cheaper Than Gas?

Driving 12,000 miles in a car that gets 30 mpg uses 400 gallons of gasoline, about $1,200 at $3 per gallon. The same miles in an average EV use about 4,000 kWh. At the national average electricity price of about 18 cents, that's around $720 from the grid. Solar electricity costs less over the system's life: an $8,500 addition producing 4,000 kWh a year for 25 years works out to under 9 cents per kWh before considering panel degradation.

When Should You Charge?

  • Full net metering: timing matters little. You can charge overnight and still offset that energy with daytime solar credits.
  • Net billing or time-of-use rates: charge midday when possible, or use a smart charger that follows solar production. Evening and peak charging is the most expensive. See NEM 3.0.
  • Battery owners: a 13.5 kWh home battery adds only about 40–50 miles of range, so it's better used for the home than for charging the car.

Home Chargers and Incentives

A Level 2 charger (240 V) adds roughly 20–40 miles of range per hour and typically costs about $1,000–$2,500 installed, more if your electrical panel needs an upgrade. Many chargers can be set to charge from surplus solar.

The federal Alternative Fuel Vehicle Refueling Property Credit (30C) for home chargers was cut short by the 2025 tax law and doesn't apply to property placed in service after June 30, 2026. Many utilities still offer charger rebates or special EV rate plans; ask yours.

Planning Tips

  1. Tell your installer about current and future EVs before the system is designed.
  2. Check whether your electrical panel can handle both solar and a 40–50 A charger.
  3. If roof space is limited, prioritize higher-efficiency panels.
  4. Consider a smart charger that can follow solar production.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I charge my EV directly from panels during an outage?

Only with a battery or backup-capable system. Standard grid-tied systems shut down during outages.

Can I add panels later when I buy an EV?

Usually yes, but it can cost more per watt than including them initially, and you may need a new interconnection approval.
