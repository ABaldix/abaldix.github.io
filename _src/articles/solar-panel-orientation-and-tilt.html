The direction your roof faces and its slope affect how much electricity your panels produce. The good news: the "perfect" roof matters less than many people think. Here is how much output you can expect from different orientations, and when it's worth adjusting the design.

Why Orientation Matters

Panels produce the most when sunlight hits them directly. In the Northern Hemisphere, the sun travels across the southern part of the sky, so south-facing panels see the most direct sun over the course of a year. "Azimuth" is the technical term for the compass direction a panel faces (180° is due south).

An east- or west-facing system typically needs one or two extra panels to match a south-facing system's output. Many homes split panels across east and west roofs, which spreads production across more of the day.

The Case for West-Facing Panels

West-facing panels produce more in the late afternoon, when electricity is often most expensive under time-of-use rates. In places with net billing, like California under NEM 3.0, that timing can make west-facing panels worth nearly as much as south-facing ones, or even more, despite producing fewer total kWh.

What About Tilt?

The ideal fixed tilt for maximum annual production is roughly equal to your latitude, which is about 25–45° across the continental US. But output changes slowly near the optimum: a roof pitched 15° or 40° usually produces within a few percent of the ideal. That's why installers almost always mount panels flush to sloped roofs instead of adding tilt frames, which cost more and catch more wind.

  • Low-slope roofs (under ~10°): lose some production and shed dirt and snow less easily; flat-roof racking can tilt panels 10–15°.
  • Steeper tilts: favor winter production and shed snow faster.
  • Flatter tilts: favor summer production.

Shade Matters More Than Direction

A perfectly south-facing roof shaded by a large tree can produce less than an unshaded east-facing roof. If part of your roof is shaded at some times of day, module-level electronics can limit the loss. See shading and optimizers.

How to Check Your Own Roof

  1. Use a phone compass or a satellite map to see which way each roof face points.
  2. Enter your address, direction, and tilt in NREL's free PVWatts calculator to estimate yearly production.
  3. Look at shade at different times of day and in winter, when the sun is lower.
  4. Ask installers for a shade analysis; most use satellite and 3D modeling tools.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a north-facing roof ever worth it?

Sometimes, on low-pitch roofs in sunny southern states, but expect much lower output. A ground mount or a different roof face is usually better.

Should I use solar trackers at home?

Trackers that follow the sun increase output but add cost and maintenance. They're rarely worth it for residential rooftops; adding a couple of extra panels is usually cheaper.
