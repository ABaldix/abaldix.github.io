Solar panels can cut your electricity bill dramatically for decades, but they are a large upfront investment, and the end of the 30% federal homeowner credit in 2026 changed the math. Whether solar is worth it for you comes down to a handful of factors you can check before you talk to a single installer.

The Five Factors That Decide Your Return

1. Your electricity rate

Every kWh your panels produce replaces a kWh you would have bought, so a higher rate means bigger savings. The US average is about 18 cents per kWh in 2026, but rates range from roughly 12 cents in states like Idaho and Utah to over 30 cents in California, Massachusetts, and Connecticut, and more than 40 cents in Hawaii. Check the price per kWh on your bill, including delivery charges.

2. How much sun your roof gets

A south-facing, unshaded roof produces the most. East- and west-facing roofs usually lose 10–20%, which is often acceptable. Heavy shade can cut output in half and is the most common reason solar doesn't pencil out. See orientation and tilt.

3. Your utility's export credit

Under classic net metering, each kWh you send to the grid earns a full retail credit. Under "net billing," as in California's NEM 3.0, exports earn much less, so you save mainly on the power you use directly or store in a battery.

4. The installed price and incentives

Prices of about $2.60–$3.00 per watt are typical in 2026. State tax credits, rebates, and SREC payments can still reduce your net cost substantially. See incentives by state.

5. How you pay

Buying with cash gives the highest lifetime savings. A loan's value depends on its interest rate and fees. A lease or PPA typically saves less over time but requires little or no money upfront, and in 2026 leasing companies can still claim a federal business credit. See lease vs. buy.

A Worked Example

Take a home using 10,800 kWh per year (about the US average) in a state with average sun and an 18-cent rate. An 8 kW system at $2.75 per watt costs $22,000 and produces roughly 10,000 kWh per year. With full net metering, that saves about $1,800 in the first year. If electricity prices rise 3% a year, cumulative savings pass $22,000 in roughly year 10–11, and the system can save $25,000–$35,000 more over its remaining warranty life.

When Solar Panels Are Probably Not Worth It

  • Your roof is heavily shaded and you can't or won't remove trees.
  • You expect to move within 5–7 years (though owned solar can add resale value).
  • Your roof needs replacement soon and you can't do both.
  • Your electricity rate is very low and your utility pays little for exports.
  • You would need an expensive loan with high dealer fees.

If rooftop solar doesn't fit, community solar subscriptions offer bill savings without installing anything, where available.

Benefits Beyond the Payback Period

Solar also protects you from future rate increases, can add value to your home, and, paired with a battery, can keep essential appliances running during outages. Those benefits don't show up in a simple payback calculation, but they matter to many homeowners.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is solar still worth it without the tax credit?

For many homes, yes, especially in high-rate states. The payback is longer than in 2025, but panels last 25–30 years, so there is usually plenty of time to recover the cost.

Will electricity prices keep rising?

Residential prices have risen faster than inflation in recent years, and the EIA expects them to keep rising in 2026. Nobody can guarantee future prices, so it's wise to test your payback with a more modest increase too.
