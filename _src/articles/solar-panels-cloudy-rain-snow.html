"Does solar even work where I live?" is a common worry, especially in cloudy or snowy states. The short answer is yes. Panels respond to light, not heat, and they keep working in a much wider range of weather than most people expect. Here is what happens in each condition.

Cloudy Days

Solar panels use both direct sunlight and diffuse light scattered by clouds and the atmosphere. On a lightly overcast day, a system might produce half its usual output; under thick, dark clouds it may drop to roughly 10–25%. Production rebounds immediately when clouds thin.

Places known for clouds still use solar successfully. Germany, which gets less sunshine than almost every US state, is one of the world's largest solar markets, and Seattle, Portland, and much of New England have thriving residential solar industries.

The "cloud edge" effect: on partly cloudy days, sunlight reflecting off the edges of clouds can briefly push panel output above its normal clear-sky level.

Rain

Rain reduces output while it falls because of the cloud cover, but it also washes dust, pollen, and bird droppings off panels, which can improve production afterward. In regions with regular rainfall, this natural cleaning is usually enough. Panels are sealed and rated for outdoor exposure, so rain itself causes no harm.

Snow

A thick layer of snow blocks light and stops production. But panels are dark, smooth, and usually tilted, so snow tends to slide off within a day or two once the sun comes out, especially as the panel warms slightly. Light snow often melts through quickly.

  • Don't climb on the roof to clear panels. It's dangerous and can void warranties.
  • A soft foam roof rake designed for panels can be used from the ground for low roofs. Never use metal tools or hot water.
  • Snow-covered ground can reflect extra light onto panels once they are clear, slightly boosting output.
  • Panels are rated for snow loads; installers check that your roof and racking can handle local snow weight.

Cold Weather Actually Helps

Solar panels become more efficient as they get colder. Panel output falls by roughly 0.3–0.4% for every degree Celsius above 25 °C (77 °F), the standard test temperature, and rises when panels are colder. A crisp, sunny winter day can produce excellent output per hour of sunlight. Winter production is lower overall mainly because days are shorter and the sun is lower in the sky.

Heat Waves

Very hot days reduce efficiency. On a 95 °F day, panel surfaces can reach 140–150 °F, cutting output by around 10–15% compared with lab conditions. Panels with a low temperature coefficient (heterojunction and back-contact cells, for example) handle heat better. See choosing panels.

Seasonal Production: What to Expect

In most of the US, a solar system produces two to three times as much in June as in December. Net metering smooths this out: summer surpluses become credits that offset winter bills. Installers size systems using typical weather data built from decades of measurements, so cloudy weeks and snowy months are already part of the estimate.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do solar panels work at night?

No. Panels need light. At night you use grid power, net metering credits, or a battery.

Do solar panels work during a power outage on a cloudy day?

Only if you have a battery or a system designed for backup. Standard grid-tied systems shut off during outages. See our outage guide.

Is solar worth it in the Pacific Northwest?

Production is lower than in sunny states, but so is the system's output per dollar spent. It depends mostly on your electricity rate and utility credits; run your address through NREL's PVWatts or our calculator.
