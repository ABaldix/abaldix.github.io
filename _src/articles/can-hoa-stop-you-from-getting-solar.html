Tens of millions of Americans live in communities governed by a homeowners association. HOAs once routinely blocked solar panels for aesthetic reasons. Today, many states limit that power, but the rules vary a lot, and even protected homeowners usually need approval first.

Not legal advice. This guide summarizes general patterns in state law. Read your HOA's governing documents and your state's statute, and consult an attorney if a dispute arises.

Is There a Federal Law?

No. Solar access is governed by state law (and sometimes local ordinances). A 2024 review by the Kansas Legislative Research Department found that 29 states had laws addressing HOA solar restrictions: 22 allow only reasonable restrictions, and 7 allow other types of limits. The remaining states had no specific law on the topic. Counts differ between sources because some laws cover only solar easements, and states continue to pass new protections.

What "Reasonable Restrictions" Usually Means

In states with solar access laws, HOAs typically cannot ban solar outright, but they can set rules about how and where panels are installed, as long as those rules don't significantly raise costs or reduce performance. Common examples:

  • Requiring architectural review and approval before installation.
  • Preferring placement on rear or side roofs when that doesn't significantly reduce output.
  • Requiring panels to sit parallel to the roofline, or wiring and conduit to be concealed or painted.
  • Requiring licensed installers and compliance with building codes.

How to Get HOA Approval for Solar Panels

  1. Read your CC&Rs and architectural guidelines. Note any solar-specific rules and the required application form.
  2. Check your state's law so you know which restrictions are allowed.
  3. Ask your installer to prepare the package: site plan, panel layout, product datasheets with photos of the all-black panels, and conduit routing.
  4. Address appearance up front: flush-mounted, all-black panels with concealed wiring usually satisfy aesthetic concerns. Some homeowners consider solar shingles; see Tesla Solar Roof vs. panels.
  5. Submit in writing and keep records of dates, and build HOA review into your installation timeline. Some states require a decision within a set period.
  6. If denied, ask for the specific reason, propose alternatives, and cite your state's statute. Many disputes resolve at this stage.

Condos and Shared Roofs

Condominium owners usually don't own their roof, so state solar access laws may not apply the same way. Options include proposing a shared system to the association, or subscribing to community solar, if available in your area.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my HOA make me put panels on a less sunny roof?

In many protected states only if the change doesn't significantly reduce performance or raise costs. The thresholds vary by state.

Can I install first and ask later?

Avoid it. Even where the law protects solar, skipping required approval can lead to fines or forced changes.
