California has some of the highest electricity prices in the country, which should make it ideal for solar. But since April 2023, new rooftop solar customers of the three big investor-owned utilities have been on a much less generous export policy known as NEM 3.0. Here is how it works and how to plan a system that still makes financial sense.

What Is NEM 3.0?

NEM 3.0 is the informal name for California's Net Billing Tariff (NBT), adopted by the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) in December 2022. It applies to new solar customers of PG&E, Southern California Edison, and San Diego Gas & Electric whose interconnection applications were submitted on or after April 15, 2023. Most municipal utilities, such as LADWP and SMUD, set their own policies.

Under the previous policy (NEM 2.0), exported solar earned close to the full retail rate. Under net billing, exports earn credits based on the utility's "avoided cost", which is what it would otherwise pay for that electricity at that hour. Those values vary by hour and month and are typically a small fraction of retail during sunny midday hours. Industry analyses put the average cut in export value at roughly 75–80%.

The Legal Fight Is Over (For Now)

Solar advocates challenged NEM 3.0 in court. In August 2025 the California Supreme Court sent the case back to the appeals court for closer review. In March 2026 the Court of Appeal again upheld the CPUC's decision, and in June 2026 the Supreme Court declined to review it further. For now, NEM 3.0 is the settled rule for new customers.

How NEM 3.0 Changes the Math

The key insight: under NEM 3.0, a kWh you use yourself is worth the full retail rate (often 35–50 cents during evening peaks), while a kWh you export at noon may be worth only a few cents. The goal is to use or store as much of your solar as possible.

Designing a System That Pays Off Under NEM 3.0

  • Add a battery. Storing midday solar and using it during the 4–9 p.m. peak captures the highest value. A single 13.5 kWh battery covers the evening peak for many homes. See battery costs.
  • Don't oversize. Panels that mostly export at low midday values add little. Weigh solar with or without a battery before you decide.
  • Shift flexible loads to midday. Run pool pumps, dishwashers, and EV charging when the sun is up. See solar and EV charging.
  • Consider west-facing panels. They produce more in the late afternoon, closer to the peak period.
  • Check SGIP. The Self-Generation Incentive Program offers battery incentives, with larger amounts for eligible low-income and medically vulnerable households, subject to funding.

Is Solar Still Worth It in California?

For many homeowners, yes. California's high retail rates mean self-consumed solar is very valuable. A well-designed solar-plus-battery system often pays back in roughly 8–12 years even without the federal credit, though results vary a lot by utility, usage pattern, and price. Solar-only systems sized for heavy export are the weakest option under NEM 3.0. Get quotes that model your actual hourly usage, not a simple annual average.

Frequently Asked Questions

I'm on NEM 2.0. Will I be moved to NEM 3.0?

Existing NEM 2.0 customers generally keep their tariff for 20 years from interconnection. Proposals to change legacy terms for homes that are sold have been debated, so watch for updates if you plan to sell.

Can I add panels to a NEM 2.0 system?

Significant expansions can affect your legacy status. Check with your utility before adding capacity.

Are NEM 3.0 export rates ever high?

Yes. Credits can spike during some late-summer evening hours. A battery set to export at those times can earn much more than midday exports.
