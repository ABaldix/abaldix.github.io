For almost twenty years, the biggest financial incentive for going solar in the US was the federal solar tax credit. Homeowners who bought a system could subtract 30% of its cost from their federal income tax. That credit is gone for systems installed in 2026. Here is exactly what changed, who can still benefit, and what incentives remain.

What Changed?

The homeowner credit was officially called the Residential Clean Energy Credit (Internal Revenue Code Section 25D). The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 had extended it at 30% through 2032, followed by a phase-down. In July 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (H.R. 1) repealed that schedule and set a hard end date of December 31, 2025, with no gradual phase-down.

"Placed in service" is what counts. The IRS generally treats a system as placed in service when installation is complete and it is ready to operate. A contract signed in 2025 for a system finished in 2026 does not qualify.

I Installed Solar in 2025. Can I Still Claim It?

Yes. If your system was completed by December 31, 2025, you claim the credit on your 2025 federal tax return by filing IRS Form 5695 with your Form 1040. The credit equals 30% of qualified costs, which include panels, inverters, racking, wiring, batteries of at least 3 kWh, labor, permitting, and sales tax.

The credit is non-refundable: it reduces the tax you owe but does not create a refund by itself. If the credit is larger than your tax bill, you can carry the unused amount forward to future years. Keep your contract, final invoice, and the utility's permission-to-operate letter, which show when the system was placed in service.

Is Any Federal Solar Tax Credit Left for Home Solar?

Indirectly, yes. The 2025 law kept a separate business credit, the Clean Electricity Investment Credit (Section 48E), which companies can claim on systems they own. When you sign a solar lease or power purchase agreement (PPA), the solar company owns the equipment on your roof and may claim that credit.

For solar, projects generally qualify if construction began by July 4, 2026, or if the system is placed in service by December 31, 2027. Batteries owned by third parties have a longer runway. You don't receive this credit yourself; some companies pass part of the saving on through lower monthly payments, but nothing guarantees it. Compare offers carefully using our lease vs. buy guide.

How Much Does Losing the Credit Cost You?

On a typical 8 kW system at $2.75 per watt ($22,000), the old credit was worth about $6,600. Losing it adds the same amount to your net cost and stretches the payback period. In an average-rate state, a payback that might have been 7–9 years with the credit is now more like 10–13 years. In high-rate states such as California, Massachusetts, or New York, solar can still pay back in under 10 years.

Incentives That Still Exist

  • State tax credits: several states, including New York, South Carolina, Massachusetts, and Arizona, offer their own credits. Caps and rules vary.
  • State and utility rebates: examples include New York's NY-Sun program and California's SGIP battery incentives.
  • Property tax exemptions: many states exclude the added value of solar from your property assessment.
  • Sales tax exemptions: many states don't charge sales tax on solar equipment.
  • SRECs and performance payments: in states such as New Jersey, Massachusetts, and Illinois, you can earn money for the solar energy you produce.
  • Net metering: credit for the excess power you send to the grid. See our net metering guide.

Watch Out for Outdated Sales Pitches

Some websites and sales materials still advertise the 30% credit for homeowner purchases. If a quote for a 2026 installation shows a "federal tax credit" line for a system you will own, ask the installer to explain it in writing. In most cases it no longer applies.

Frequently Asked Questions

Could Congress bring the credit back?

It is possible but not scheduled. Base your decision on the incentives that exist today, and treat any future credit as a bonus.

Do batteries still get a federal credit?

Not for homeowners who buy them in 2026. Batteries owned by a leasing company may still qualify for the business credit.

What about the Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit (25C)?

That separate credit for upgrades such as heat pumps and insulation was also ended by the 2025 law for property placed in service after 2025. Check IRS guidance for details.
