Going solar is one of the largest purchases most homeowners make, and the rules changed in 2026. This guide walks through the whole decision in order: how a home system works, how much it costs, which equipment matters, whether you need a battery, which incentives are left, and what installation looks like. Each section links to a deeper guide when you want more detail.

How a Home Solar System Works

A rooftop system has four main parts. Solar panels turn sunlight into direct current (DC) electricity. An inverter converts that DC power into the alternating current (AC) your home uses. Racking attaches the panels to your roof, and a monitoring system shows how much energy you produce.

Most US systems are grid-tied. During the day your home uses solar power first. When the panels produce more than you need, the extra flows to the utility grid and your utility credits you for it under a net metering or net billing program. At night, or on cloudy days, you draw power from the grid as usual. A grid-tied system without a battery shuts off during a blackout for safety reasons, which we explain in our power outage guide.

Step 1: Check Whether Your Home Is a Good Fit

Before you collect quotes, look at four things:

  • Roof orientation and shade. South-facing roofs produce the most in the US, but east and west roofs typically lose only 10–20%. Heavy shade from trees or nearby buildings is a bigger problem. See orientation and tilt and shading.
  • Roof age. Panels last 25–30 years. If your roof needs replacing within the next 5–10 years, it is usually cheaper to replace it before installing solar.
  • Electricity rate. Look at the price per kWh on your bill. The US average is about 18 cents per kWh in 2026, but rates range from about 12 cents to over 40 cents depending on the state.
  • Local rules. Your utility's export credit policy and your HOA (if you have one) both affect the project. See HOA rules.

Step 2: Size the System

System size depends on how much electricity you use, not on your home's square footage. Add up the kWh from your last 12 bills and divide by your area's annual production per kW. In much of the US, 1 kW of panels produces roughly 1,200–1,500 kWh per year.

Step 3: Understand the Cost

Installed prices in 2026 generally fall between $2.60 and $3.00 per watt before incentives, with large marketplace averages near $2.60–$2.85. That puts a typical 8 kW system at about $21,000–$23,000. Prices are higher in some Northeast states and for premium equipment, and lower in competitive markets.

The biggest change this year: the 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit ended on December 31, 2025. Homeowners who buy a system in 2026 pay the full price unless a state or utility program helps. Read what changed with the tax credit and a full cost breakdown for a typical home.

Step 4: Choose How to Pay

You have three main options:

  • Cash: the lowest lifetime cost and the highest long-term savings.
  • Solar loan: little or no money down; savings depend on the interest rate. Watch for high "dealer fees" built into low advertised rates.
  • Lease or PPA: a company owns the system and you pay a monthly fee or a per-kWh rate. Leasing companies can still claim a federal business credit, which can make leases more competitive in 2026. See lease vs. buy.

Step 5: Pick the Equipment

Nearly all residential panels sold today are monocrystalline, with efficiencies around 20–23%. For most roofs, a reputable mid-range panel with a 25-year warranty is the best value; premium panels make sense mainly when roof space is limited. See the best solar panels for homes and monocrystalline panels explained.

For inverters, the main choice is between a single string inverter (lower cost) and microinverters or optimizers (better with shade and panel-level monitoring). If you want a battery now or later, a hybrid inverter or a battery with a built-in inverter is worth considering. Compare them in microinverters vs. string inverters and hybrid vs. grid-tie inverters.

Step 6: Decide on a Battery

A battery adds about $10,000–$16,000 for 10–14 kWh of storage. It makes the most sense if you want backup power during outages, if your utility pays little for exported solar (as in California), or if you're on time-of-use rates. Read solar with or without a battery and how much a solar battery costs.

Step 7: Find Incentives

With the federal homeowner credit gone, state programs matter more. Some states offer tax credits or rebates, many exempt solar from property or sales tax, and a few have markets for solar renewable energy certificates (SRECs). See our state incentives guide.

Step 8: Compare Quotes and Install

Get at least three quotes. Compare price per watt, the exact panel and inverter models, the production estimate, and warranties for equipment and workmanship. Our quote comparison checklist lists the red flags to watch for.

After you sign, expect about 1–3 months before the system is turned on. Most of that time goes to design, permits, inspection, and utility approval; the physical installation usually takes one to three days. See the installation process step by step.

Is Solar Worth It for You?

Solar is usually a good investment when you plan to stay in your home for at least 10 years, your electricity rate is above the national average, your roof gets good sun, and your utility offers fair credit for exported power. Without the federal credit, typical payback periods are now roughly 9–14 years, shorter in high-rate states with strong incentives. Frequently Asked Questions

How long do solar panels last?

Most panels carry 25- to 30-year warranties and keep working beyond that, losing roughly 0.5% of their output per year. Inverters usually need replacing sooner. See how long solar panels last.

Do solar panels work on cloudy days?

Yes, at reduced output. Many cloudy places, including Germany and the Pacific Northwest, still use solar successfully. See solar in clouds, rain, and snow.

Will solar panels increase my home's value?

Studies have found a price premium for homes with owned solar systems, though the size varies by market. Leased systems can complicate a sale. See solar and home value.
