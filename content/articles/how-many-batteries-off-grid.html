Going completely off the grid is an appealing idea, but it changes the battery math entirely. A grid-tied home with backup needs enough storage for an occasional outage. An off-grid home needs enough to get through every night, every cloudy week, and the darkest days of winter. Here's how to work out how many batteries you need to go off-grid.

Step 1: Calculate Daily Energy Use

Divide your annual kWh by 365. The average US home uses about 29–30 kWh per day. Off-grid homes typically cut that dramatically by switching to efficient appliances, using propane or wood for heating and cooking, and avoiding electric resistance heat. Many off-grid homes run on 8–15 kWh per day.

Step 2: Choose Days of Autonomy

"Days of autonomy" is how many days the batteries can carry your home with little or no solar input. Two to three days is common in sunny climates; three to five or more in cloudy or snowy ones, unless a generator covers longer gaps.

Step 3: Account for Usable Capacity and Losses

Modern lithium (LFP) batteries list "usable" capacity, but it's still wise to avoid running them to empty routinely, and inverters lose some energy. A practical approach is to add about 15–20% on top of your calculated need.

Battery capacity ≈ daily kWh × days of autonomy × 1.2

At roughly $850–$1,300 per installed kWh, 72 kWh of storage alone would cost about $60,000–$95,000. That's why most off-grid designs focus on reducing usage first.

Step 4: Size the Solar Array for Winter

An off-grid array must recharge the batteries in the shortest, cloudiest days, not just on average. In much of the US, December production is one-third to one-half of June's, so off-grid arrays are often 1.5–2 times larger than a grid-tied system for the same home. Check winter production for your location with NREL's PVWatts.

Step 5: Plan for a Generator

Almost every off-grid home keeps a generator for long cloudy stretches. Covering a rare week of storms with a generator is far cheaper than buying enough batteries to handle it. For small cabins, a large portable power station can also fill gaps.

Power Matters Too, Not Just Energy

Batteries and inverters are rated in kW (power) as well as kWh (energy). Well pumps, air conditioners, and shop tools have high startup surges. Make sure your inverter and battery bank can handle your largest loads running at the same time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it legal to go off-grid?

Generally yes, but local building codes and permits still apply, and some municipalities require a grid connection for occupied homes. Check with your local building department.

Can I keep the grid as a backup instead?

Yes, and for most people it's the smarter choice: a grid-tied system with a backup battery delivers most of the resilience at a fraction of the cost.
