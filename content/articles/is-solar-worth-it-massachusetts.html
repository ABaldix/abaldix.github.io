Is solar worth it in Massachusetts? It is one of the strongest cases in the country. Massachusetts homeowners pay more than 30¢ per kWh on average, the big utilities still offer full retail net metering for home systems, and the state layers a production incentive, a tax credit, and two tax exemptions on top. The sun is modest, but the savings per kWh are large.

How SMART 3.0 Works

The Solar Massachusetts Renewable Target (SMART) program pays you for the electricity your system produces, on top of your net metering savings. SMART 3.0, which began accepting 2026 applications on January 1, 2026, uses a simple flat rate for most residential systems of 25 kW or less: $0.03 per kWh in 2026, or $0.06 per kWh for income-qualified households. According to MassCEC, the rate is locked for 20 years once you qualify, and you can choose a check or a bill credit.

For a system producing 10,800 kWh a year, the base rate is worth about $320 a year. The Department of Energy Resources resets rates and capacity each program year, so the rate offered to new applicants can change.

Massachusetts Solar Incentives in 2026

  • Residential renewable energy income tax credit: 15% of the cost, up to $1,000, with carryforward if you cannot use it all in one year.
  • Sales tax exemption: solar equipment is exempt from the 6.25% state sales tax.
  • Property tax exemption: the added value of a solar system is exempt from local property tax for 20 years.
  • Net metering: Eversource, National Grid, and Unitil must offer it for systems with inverters under 25 kW. Some municipal light plants have their own rules.
  • ConnectedSolutions: Mass Save utilities pay battery owners for discharging during summer peak events, which can cover a meaningful share of a battery's cost over time. SMART 3.0 also includes adders for some storage-paired systems.

Winter, Snow, and Roofs

Massachusetts systems produce far less in December than in June, but net metering banks summer surpluses for winter bills. Snow typically slides off within a few sunny days, and cold panels are more efficient. The bigger issues are older roofs, tree shade, and historic-district rules in some towns. Who Should Go Solar in Massachusetts

Almost any homeowner with a reasonably sunny roof and a few years of state tax liability is a good candidate. If you are served by a municipal light plant, check its export rules first. If your roof is shaded or you rent, community solar can still deliver a discount on your bill.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Massachusetts still have net metering?

Yes. The three regulated utilities must offer full retail net metering for residential-size systems. Municipal light plants can set different rules.

How much does SMART 3.0 pay?

For most residential systems in 2026, $0.03 per kWh produced, or $0.06 for income-qualified households, locked in for 20 years once you qualify.

Is there a Massachusetts solar tax credit?

Yes. The state credit equals 15% of the cost, up to $1,000. It is separate from the federal homeowner credit, which ended after 2025.

How long does solar take to pay off in Massachusetts?

Typically about 6 to 8 years for a purchased system in 2026, depending on your installed price and electricity rate.
