If you have looked at solar quotes, you have almost certainly seen the word "monocrystalline." Monocrystalline solar panels are named for how their silicon is made, and they are now the default for home solar. Here is what it means, how it compares with other panel types, and which newer cell technologies are worth knowing about.

How Monocrystalline Solar Panels Are Made

Monocrystalline cells are cut from a single, continuous crystal of very pure silicon, grown as a cylindrical ingot and sliced into thin wafers. Because the crystal structure is uniform, electrons move through it more easily, which gives higher efficiency than silicon made of many small crystals. You can recognize monocrystalline panels by their uniform black or very dark blue cells.

Polycrystalline panels used to be the cheaper option, but as monocrystalline manufacturing scaled up, the price gap mostly disappeared. Today nearly all residential panels sold in the US are monocrystalline. Thin-film technologies such as cadmium telluride dominate some utility-scale projects but are uncommon on homes.

The Cell Technologies Inside Modern Panels

"Monocrystalline" describes the silicon. Most of today's efficiency gains come from the cell design built on that silicon:

  • PERC (Passivated Emitter and Rear Cell): the mainstream design of the late 2010s and early 2020s, now being replaced.
  • TOPCon (Tunnel Oxide Passivated Contact): an "n-type" design that has become the new mainstream, with higher efficiency and lower degradation than PERC.
  • Heterojunction (HJT): combines crystalline silicon with thin amorphous layers; excellent heat performance and low degradation. REC's Alpha series uses it.
  • Back-contact (IBC/ABC): moves all electrical contacts to the back so the front captures more light. Used by Maxeon, Aiko, and LONGi's top models, with module efficiencies reaching about 24–25%.

You'll also see half-cut cells (cells split in two to reduce losses and improve shade tolerance) and bifacial panels (which collect light from both sides, mainly useful on ground mounts).

Advantages and Disadvantages

Advantages

  • Highest efficiency for residential use, so you need fewer panels and less roof space.
  • Long lifespans, typically warranted for 25–30 years or more.
  • Good performance in heat and low light with modern n-type cells.

Disadvantages

  • The highest-efficiency models cost noticeably more per watt.
  • Rigid glass panels are heavy (about 45–55 lbs each) compared with flexible products.

Are There Still Tax Credits for Monocrystalline Panels?

Panel technology has never affected eligibility for incentives. What changed is the incentive itself: the 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit ended on December 31, 2025, so homeowners who buy a system in 2026 get no federal credit, whichever panel they choose. State programs still apply; see incentives by state.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is monocrystalline worth the extra cost over polycrystalline?

In 2026 there is little or no extra cost, and polycrystalline panels are hard to find for homes. Monocrystalline is the default choice.

What does "n-type" mean?

It refers to how the silicon is doped. N-type cells (TOPCon, HJT, most back-contact) resist certain kinds of early degradation better than older p-type PERC cells.
