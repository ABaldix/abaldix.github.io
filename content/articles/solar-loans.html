In 2023, 58% of home solar projects were paid for with loans, according to the CFPB, and many borrowers never see the full cost. A loan advertised at 1.99% can be more expensive than one at 7.5%, depending on fees and on how long you keep it. This guide explains how solar loans work in 2026 and how to compare them on equal terms.

How Solar Loans Work

There are two broad ways to borrow for solar:

  • Installer-arranged solar loans. The salesperson signs you up with a specialist lender at the kitchen table. These loans are usually fixed-rate, run 10 to 25 years, and often need no down payment. Many are unsecured, though lenders commonly record a fixture filing on the equipment.
  • Financing you arrange yourself. A credit union or bank solar loan, a home equity loan, a home equity line of credit (HELOC), or a personal loan. To the installer you are a cash customer.

Dealer Fees: The Hidden Cost

Lenders keep advertised rates low by charging the installer a dealer fee, also called a platform, program, or origination fee. The installer passes it on by raising the price of the system, so you borrow more than the cash price. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) reported in 2024 that these fees can increase the loan amount by 30% or more above the cash price, and that they are usually not included in the stated APR. Typical fees range from about 10% to 30%, and lower rates generally mean higher fees.

The effect shows up in national data. Berkeley Lab's latest update found that loan-financed home solar systems had a median price of $4.50 per watt in 2025, compared with $3.00 per watt for systems bought with cash.

The lesson is not that dealer-fee loans are always worse. Kept for the full term, a very low rate can cost less overall. But the fee is paid on day one, so if you sell your home or refinance in the first few years, you lose it. The fee also makes it harder to see the real price of the system. Always ask for the cash price and the dealer fee in writing, then compare the total cost over the number of years you expect to keep the loan.

The Tax-Credit Re-Amortization Trap

For years, many solar loans were designed around the 30% federal tax credit. The payment was set as if you would put your tax refund toward the loan, often within about 18 months. If you didn't, the loan re-amortized and the monthly payment rose. The CFPB flagged this structure as a risk to borrowers.

The homeowner credit ended for systems installed after December 31, 2025, so a loan for a system you buy in 2026 should not assume a 30% paydown. If an offer still mentions a "tax credit payment," a "re-amortization date," or a payment that changes after the first year, ask exactly what the payment will be if you make no lump-sum payment. Is a HELOC Better for Solar?

A HELOC or home equity loan lets you pay the installer in cash, so there is no dealer fee and you can negotiate as a cash buyer. Rates were around 7–8% in late 2026. The downsides: your home secures the debt, HELOC rates are usually variable, and there may be closing costs. A HELOC works best for homeowners with plenty of equity and a disciplined repayment plan.

Does a Solar Loan Pay for Itself?

Compare the monthly payment with your expected bill savings, not with your whole current bill. Fixed charges stay on your bill, and in many states exported solar earns far less than retail. In the example above, an 8 kW system in a state with average prices might save about $150–$170 a month in its first year, so the payment on a no-fee 20-year loan would exceed the savings at first, while the low-APR loan would be lower. Interest also lengthens payback compared with paying cash. Check what solar is worth in your state in our solar by state guides and run your own numbers in the solar calculator.

Loan, Cash, or Lease?

Cash usually gives the best lifetime return. A fair loan lets you own the system and its value without paying upfront. In 2026, leases and PPAs have also become more competitive, because leasing companies can still claim a federal business credit that homeowners cannot. Solar Loan Checklist

  • Get the cash price and the loan amount in writing; the difference is the fee.
  • Compare total cost over the years you expect to keep the loan.
  • Check for prepayment penalties and payment changes after the first year.
  • Ask whether the lender will file a lien or fixture filing, and how that is handled when you sell.
  • Get a quote from a credit union or your bank before signing at the kitchen table.
  • Choose the installer first, then the financing. See how to choose a solar installer.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a dealer fee on a solar loan?

A fee the lender charges the installer to offer a low interest rate. It is usually added to the system price, so you borrow more than the cash price. The CFPB found such fees can raise the loan 30% or more above the cash price.

What is a good interest rate for a solar loan in 2026?

No-fee loans and HELOCs were generally around 6–9% in late 2026. Rates below about 4% almost always come with a dealer fee, so compare total cost rather than the rate alone.

Can I still use the tax credit to pay down my solar loan?

Not for a system you buy in 2026. The 30% homeowner credit ended for systems installed after December 31, 2025, so make sure your loan payment does not assume it.

Is it better to finance solar with a HELOC?

It can be, because you avoid dealer fees and buy as a cash customer. But your home secures the debt and rates are usually variable.
