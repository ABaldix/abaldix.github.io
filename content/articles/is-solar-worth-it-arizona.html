Is solar worth it in Arizona? The answer is more nuanced than the sunshine suggests. Arizona panels produce more power than anywhere else in the country, but the state ended retail net metering in 2017. Today, APS and TEP customers are paid a modest export rate for surplus power, and SRP uses its own solar price plans. Solar still saves money for most homeowners, but the best results come from using as much of your own power as possible.

How Net Billing Works at APS, TEP, and SRP

Under net billing, power your home uses as it is produced offsets your full retail price, while surplus power sent to the grid earns a separate export credit.

  • APS pays the Resource Comparison Proxy (RCP) export rate, which was about 6.2¢ per kWh for customers who applied between September 2025 and August 2026. Your rate is locked for 10 years from when you apply, and the rate offered to new applicants can drop by up to 10% every September 1.
  • TEP uses a similar export rate, which was about 5.7¢ per kWh for 2025–2026.
  • SRP, a public power utility, sets its own solar price plans, and some include demand charges based on your highest usage. Model these carefully with your installer.

Because exports earn roughly 40% of retail, every kWh you use yourself is worth about two and a half times more than one you export. That changes how you should design the system.

How to Make Solar Pay More in Arizona

  • Size to your daytime use, not your annual total. A smaller system that exports less can deliver a better return than one sized to cover 100% of your bill.
  • Consider west-facing panels. They produce more in the late afternoon, when air conditioning runs hardest and on-peak prices apply. See orientation and tilt.
  • Shift loads into solar hours. Pre-cool the house, run the pool pump, and charge an EV at midday.
  • Pick heat-tolerant panels. Panels lose output as they heat up. A better temperature coefficient pays off on Arizona roofs; our REC vs. Qcells comparison explains why.
  • Batteries are a closer call. In our model, adding a $14,000 battery stretched payback to about 16 years. Storage makes more sense if you value backup power, face demand charges, or join a utility battery program.

Arizona Solar Incentives in 2026

  • Credit for Solar Energy Devices. 25% of the cost, up to a $1,000 lifetime maximum, with unused credit carried forward for up to five years.
  • Sales tax exemption. Qualifying solar equipment is exempt from state transaction privilege tax.
  • Property tax exemption. The added value of a solar system is not assessed for property tax.
  • No federal credit for purchases. It ended for homeowners after 2025. Leases and PPAs can still pass through the commercial credit; see lease vs. buy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Arizona have net metering?

No. Arizona replaced retail net metering with net billing in 2017. Utilities credit exported power at an export rate that is well below the retail price.

Should I lock in the APS export rate now?

APS locks your export rate for 10 years based on when you apply, and the rate for new applicants can fall each September. If you are ready to buy, applying earlier secures a slightly higher rate, but do not rush a bad quote to catch it.

Do solar panels work well in Arizona heat?

Yes, though output drops a little on very hot days. Panels with a low temperature coefficient and good airflow under the array keep more of their output in summer.

How much do solar panels cost in Arizona?

Many Arizona quotes come in around $2.50 to $2.90 per watt in 2026, so a 6 to 8 kW system typically costs about $15,000 to $23,000 before the state credit.
