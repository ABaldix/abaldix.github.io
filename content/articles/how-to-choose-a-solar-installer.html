A solar system should run for 25 years or more, and the company that installs it decides how well it is wired, how your roof is sealed, and who answers the phone when something fails. Price matters, but how to choose a solar installer comes down to a few checks that take less than an hour and filter out most bad deals.

How to Choose a Solar Installer: 6 Checks

1. Check the License

There is no national solar license. Each state sets its own rules: some require an electrical contractor's license, some a general or specialty solar contractor license, and some both. California, for example, uses C-46 (solar) and C-10 (electrical) licenses, while Texas requires a state electrical contractor license. Ask for the license number and look it up on your state licensing board's website. Confirm it is active, in the company's name, and that there are no open disciplinary actions.

2. Confirm Insurance and Bonding

The U.S. Department of Energy advises hiring an installer that is licensed, bonded, and insured to install home solar where you live. Ask for certificates of general liability insurance and workers' compensation coverage. If a worker is injured on your roof or your house is damaged, you do not want to be the one paying.

3. Look for NABCEP Certification and Experience

The North American Board of Certified Energy Practitioners (NABCEP) runs the most widely recognized certification for solar professionals. It is not legally required in most states, but a company with at least one NABCEP-certified installer or designer has shown it knows the trade. You can search the NABCEP directory by name. DOE also suggests working with installers that have at least three years of solar experience.

4. Find Out Who Actually Does the Work

Many solar companies are sales organizations that hand the installation to subcontractors. That is not automatically bad, but you should know:

  • Who will be on your roof, and whether they are employees or subcontractors. DOE recommends checking subcontractors' credentials too.
  • Who handles permits, inspection, and utility interconnection. A local installer that knows your utility's process usually gets you switched on faster. See the installation timeline.
  • Who you call for service in year 5 or year 15.

5. Check the Warranties and Who Backs Them

A solar system usually has three separate warranties: the panel manufacturer's product and performance warranties, the inverter warranty, and the installer's workmanship warranty covering wiring, mounting, and roof penetrations. Look for a workmanship warranty of at least 10 years.

Manufacturer warranties survive if your installer goes out of business, but the workmanship warranty usually does not. Several large solar companies have gone bankrupt in recent years, including SunPower in 2024, leaving customers without the service they were promised. Ask how long the company has been operating, whether its workmanship warranty is backed by third-party insurance, and whether the panels and inverters come from manufacturers with a long track record. See how long solar panels last.

6. Read Reviews the Right Way

Look at reviews on several sites, and read the negative ones. A few complaints are normal; watch for patterns such as unfinished jobs, missed interconnection deadlines, roof leaks, or unanswered warranty claims. Search the company name with "complaint" and "lawsuit," check your state attorney general's consumer complaints if available, and ask for references from customers who installed at least a year ago.

Questions to Ask Every Solar Installer

  • What is your license number, and can you send your insurance certificates?
  • Who on your team is NABCEP-certified?
  • Do you use your own crews or subcontractors?
  • What is the exact panel and inverter model, and why did you choose it?
  • What is the cash price, and what is the price per watt?
  • How did you estimate my first-year production, and does it match NREL's PVWatts?
  • What does your workmanship warranty cover, for how long, and what happens if you close?
  • Who handles roof leaks related to the installation?
  • How long from signing to switch-on, and what are the payment milestones?

Red Flags That Should End the Conversation

  • Pressure to sign today, or "limited-time" pricing that expires tonight.
  • "Free solar" or "government program" pitches. These are usually leases, PPAs, or loans described in a misleading way.
  • Claims to work for your utility. Utilities do not send solar salespeople door to door.
  • A 30% federal tax credit on a 2026 purchase. The homeowner credit ended for systems installed after December 31, 2025.
  • Only a monthly payment, no cash price. Financing fees can add thousands; see solar loans.
  • Savings that assume electricity prices rise 5% or more every year, or a promise to "eliminate" your bill.
  • Vague equipment such as "Tier 1 panels" with no model numbers.
  • Large upfront deposits before permits are approved.

If you sign a contract at home, the FTC's Cooling-Off Rule generally gives you until midnight of the third business day to cancel sales of $25 or more made at your home, and some states give more time. Read the cancellation section before you sign.

Local Installer or National Company?

Both can work. Established local installers often know your utility and permit office well and may offer more personal service. National companies may offer more financing options and standardized warranties, but some rely heavily on subcontractors and door-to-door sales. Frequently Asked Questions

Do solar installers need a license?

Yes, in almost every state, but the type of license varies. It may be an electrical contractor license, a general contractor license, or a solar-specific license. Check with your state licensing board.

Is NABCEP certification required?

Usually not by law, but it is the most widely recognized solar credential and a good sign of expertise. Some state programs require it for rebates.

How many quotes should I get?

At least three, ideally including one established local installer, so you can compare price per watt, equipment, and warranties.

What happens to my warranty if the installer goes out of business?

Panel and inverter warranties stay with the manufacturers, but the installer's workmanship warranty usually ends unless it is backed by insurance or taken over by another company.
